The quiet collapse of local newsrooms

Between 2005 and 2024, the United States lost 2,886 newspapers — most of them weeklies in counties of fewer than 50,000 people. The closures don't just shrink coverage. They reshape turnout, bond ratings, and trust.

The pattern is geographic and it is structural. Of the 3,143 counties in the country, 206 now have no local newspaper at all and another 1,562 have only one — typically a weekly with fewer than three full-time reporters. The losses concentrate in places already losing population, jobs, and broadband. News deserts compound on the deserts that produced them.

"The first thing that goes is the school-board meeting. The last thing that goes is the obituary page. Everything in between is what we used to call accountability."
— Penelope Muse Abernathy, UNC Hussman
U.S. newspapers in operation, 2005–2024
A net loss of one paper every 2.6 days for nineteen years.

What replaces the local paper, when anything does, is rarely local. Roughly 63% of news desert counties are now covered — to whatever extent they are covered — by regional digital outlets headquartered more than 100 miles away. Nearly half of those outlets have no reporter who has ever been to the county.

The downstream signals are starting to show up in unexpected ledgers. Counties that lost their last newspaper between 2008 and 2018 paid, on average, 11 basis points more on subsequent municipal bond issues than demographically matched peers. The cost of explaining a town to itself, it turns out, is also the cost of borrowing.
